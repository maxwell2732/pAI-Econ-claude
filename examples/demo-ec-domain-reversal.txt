mode: empirical-companion

Empirical question:
Does expanding rural broadband coverage raise off-farm labor supply among rural household
members?

Baseline result:
Household members in villages that received broadband raise off-farm hours worked by about
6.4% relative to not-yet-connected villages (staggered rollout, Table 2, significant at 1%).

Proposed mechanism:
Broadband lowers the cost of finding off-farm work. Job search requires time and effort;
online listings and messaging platforms reduce the effort cost per contact, which lowers the
marginal cost of search and raises the equilibrium search intensity. Higher search intensity
raises the job-finding rate and therefore off-farm hours.

Mechanism test:
We measure self-reported job-search time and the number of employer contacts per month from
a supplementary survey module. Both rise after connection, and the rise in off-farm hours is
concentrated among households that report using online job search (Table 4).

Heterogeneity results:
The effect is larger for younger household members and for households with at least one
member who completed middle school. It is near zero for households where all adults have
primary schooling or less (Table 6).

Key hypotheses to rationalize:
H1: Broadband access raises off-farm hours worked.
H2: The effect operates through a lower marginal cost of search — it is larger where search
    costs fall more.
H3: The effect is larger for households with higher schooling.

Preferred theoretical tradition, if any:
A search model with costly search effort and a household labor allocation decision.

Scope exclusions:
No general equilibrium effects on the local wage. No migration decision. No firm-side
vacancy posting; treat the arrival rate as a function of search intensity alone. No dynamic
human capital accumulation.

Declared empirical domain:
Search cost reductions in the sample correspond to a fall of between 10% and 45% in the
per-contact effort cost. Off-farm wage premia over farm work are strictly positive for all
households in the sample. Household members supply strictly positive farm hours, so no
household is at the corner of full off-farm specialization. Reservation wages lie strictly
below the off-farm wage.

Note for the pipeline:
We are aware that in a search model a large enough fall in search cost can raise the
reservation wage enough to lower accepted-offer rates. Our sample does not contain search
cost reductions anywhere near that large. Record any such reversal, but do not let a
boundary case that our data cannot reach take over the theory section.
